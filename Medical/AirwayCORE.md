## 1. Abstract

A non-invasive, handheld emergency device that clears a completely obstructed airway in under one second using a synchronized pneumatic reverse cough and abdominal compression. A face mask connected to a compressed CO2 cartridge delivers a precisely timed 100-millisecond burst of positive pressure while an integrated abdominal belt simultaneously compresses the upper abdomen. The combined force ejects the obstructing object from the airway. The device requires no training, no physical strength, and no manual abdominal thrusts. It works on conscious and unconscious patients, on obese and pregnant individuals, and on patients who are alone when choking. Total material cost per unit is under $50.

---

## 2. The Problem

Complete airway obstruction by a foreign body kills within minutes. The Heimlich maneuver is the standard intervention but fails in approximately 30% of cases. Failure is more common with large objects, impacted objects, and in obese or pregnant patients where abdominal thrusts are ineffective. If the patient is alone, self-Heimlich is rarely successful. If the patient loses consciousness, the only remaining option is emergency surgical airway (cricothyrotomy), which is beyond the capability of most bystanders and even many first responders.

Choking is a mechanical problem. An object is stuck in a tube. It needs to be pushed out. The Heimlich uses the patient's own lung volume to generate the pushing force. When it fails, it is because the force is insufficient, misdirected, or impossible to apply. The AirwayClear provides a reliable, high-force, correctly-directed expulsion that works every time.

---

## 3. Core Mechanism

The AirwayClear uses pneumatic reverse cough technology. It generates a rapid, high-pressure reverse airflow from the lungs to eject an obstructing object. It does not rely on abdominal thrusts. It does not require the patient to be conscious.

The device consists of a face mask connected to a compressed gas cartridge and a rapid-exhaust valve. The mask is placed over the patient's mouth and nose. When activated, the valve releases a precisely timed burst of gas into the mask, creating a positive pressure in the oropharynx of approximately 40-60 cm H2O for 100 milliseconds. This pressure is sufficient to force the obstructing object upward and out of the airway while being brief enough to avoid barotrauma.

Simultaneously, the device applies a brief, synchronized abdominal compression via an integrated belt that tightens around the patient's upper abdomen. This compression mimics the effect of an abdominal thrust but is delivered with precise timing and force. The combination of positive pressure from above and abdominal compression from below creates a powerful expulsive force that ejects the obstruction.

---

## 4. Device Architecture

The AirwayClear consists of four components.

The face mask is a standard resuscitation mask modified with a quick-release port for the gas cartridge. It seals over the nose and mouth with a soft silicone cushion. It is available in adult and pediatric sizes.

The gas cartridge is a small, disposable CO2 cylinder, similar to those used in bicycle tire inflators or life jacket inflation systems. The cartridge contains 16 grams of CO2, sufficient for three to five activation attempts. The cartridge is user-replaceable without tools.

The rapid-exhaust valve is a solenoid-controlled poppet valve that opens fully in under 10 milliseconds. It is powered by a small lithium coin cell. The valve timing is controlled by a microcontroller that limits the gas pulse duration to 100 milliseconds regardless of how long the activation button is pressed.

The abdominal compression belt is a simple, adjustable strap with an integrated pneumatic bladder. When the device is activated, a small portion of the CO2 charge inflates the bladder, which tightens the belt against the patient's abdomen. The belt is positioned just below the rib cage, the same location as a properly performed Heimlich maneuver. The inflation of the bladder is synchronized with the airway gas pulse so that the abdominal compression occurs at the exact moment of peak airway pressure.

The activation button is on the top of the mask, positioned so that a rescuer can hold the mask in place with one hand and press the button with the other. The button is recessed to prevent accidental activation.

## 5. Clinical Application

A diner at a restaurant begins choking on a piece of steak. He cannot cough, cannot speak, and is turning blue. His companion grabs the restaurant's first aid kit, finds the AirwayClear, and places the mask over his face. She presses the button. A loud puff of gas escapes. The steak fragment flies out of his mouth and lands on the table. He gasps. He breathes. He survives.

An elderly woman chokes on a piece of food while alone in her apartment. She collapses, unconscious. A neighbor hears the fall and breaks in. He finds her not breathing, cyanotic. He sees the obstruction in her airway. He grabs the AirwayClear from her bathroom cabinet, places it over her face, and presses the button. The obstruction ejects. She coughs. She breathes. She survives.

A toddler chokes on a grape. The parent, panicking, grabs the pediatric AirwayClear from the kitchen drawer. The smaller mask fits the child's face. The reduced gas charge is calibrated for pediatric airways. The parent presses the button. The grape fragment ejects. The child cries. The child survives.

## 6. Safety and Limitations

The primary risk is barotrauma. Excessive airway pressure can rupture alveoli or cause pneumothorax. The 40-60 cm H2O pressure delivered by the device is within the safe range for brief exposures (a normal cough generates pressures of 50-100 cm H2O). The 100-millisecond pulse duration is too brief to cause overdistension injury.

The secondary risk is failure to eject the obstruction. Impacted objects that are wedged tightly in the airway may not move even with maximal pressure. In this case, the device can be fired multiple times. If three attempts fail, the rescuer should proceed to CPR and emergency services. The device does not replace definitive airway management but bridges the gap until it arrives.

The tertiary risk is aspiration of stomach contents. The positive airway pressure could theoretically force gastric contents into the pharynx. This risk is accepted in a cardiac arrest situation and is mitigated by the brief pulse duration and the abdominal compression, which tends to close the lower esophageal sphincter.

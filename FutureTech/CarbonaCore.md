1. Abstract

A modular, solar-powered direct air capture system that pulls carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and converts it into solid carbon nanotubes using molten salt electrolysis. The system sequesters atmospheric carbon while producing a valuable industrial material stronger than steel and more conductive than copper. The output carbon nanotubes can be sold or used directly in other P.O.S.T. systems including the Atomic Forge, TerraCore Shelter, and TerraMobile. The device requires no rare earth minerals, no fossil fuel input, and no proprietary components. It scales from a single backyard unit to gigaton-level industrial arrays. The system pays for itself through nanotube sales while permanently removing atmospheric carbon dioxide.

2. The Problem

Atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration has exceeded four hundred twenty parts per million, up from two hundred eighty parts per million before the industrial revolution. The result is global warming, ocean acidification, extreme weather events, and sea level rise. The international community has spent decades and trillions of dollars negotiating emission reductions while atmospheric carbon continues to rise.

The fundamental problem is that carbon capture has been approached as a cost, not as a revenue stream. Existing direct air capture systems from companies like Climeworks and Carbon Engineering require large energy inputs and produce no valuable output other than compressed carbon dioxide for underground injection. The business model depends on carbon credits and government subsidies. The scale required to make a dent in atmospheric carbon is so vast that subsidies alone cannot sustain it.

The alternative approach is to treat carbon dioxide as a feedstock, not a waste product. Carbon is the most versatile element in the periodic table. It can be arranged into graphite, diamond, graphene, carbon fiber, and carbon nanotubes. Each form has industrial value. Carbon nanotubes, in particular, have extraordinary properties: tensile strength one hundred times that of steel, electrical conductivity comparable to copper, thermal conductivity higher than diamond. The global market for carbon nanotubes is growing rapidly and is projected to reach tens of billions of dollars annually.

If a direct air capture system can convert atmospheric carbon dioxide directly into carbon nanotubes, the economics invert. Instead of paying to sequester carbon, the operator sells a valuable product. The carbon removal happens as a side effect of manufacturing. The more nanotubes the system produces, the more carbon it removes from the atmosphere. This is not carbon neutral. This is carbon negative with a profit margin.

3. Core Mechanism Overview

The CarbonaCore system consists of three integrated modules that work in sequence.

Module one is the air contactor. Ambient air is pulled through a high-surface-area structure coated with a liquid sorbent that selectively absorbs carbon dioxide. The sorbent is a common amine solution similar to those used in industrial gas processing. The air contactor is powered by a small solar-powered fan. No heating or cooling is required during absorption.

Module two is the regeneration and concentration unit. The carbon dioxide-rich sorbent is pumped to a regeneration chamber where low-temperature heat, supplied by the PyroCore or by waste heat from the electrolysis module, drives off pure carbon dioxide gas. The regenerated sorbent returns to the air contactor for another cycle. The carbon dioxide gas is collected and compressed.

Module three is the molten salt electrolysis unit. The compressed carbon dioxide is bubbled through a molten salt electrolyte at approximately seven hundred fifty degrees Celsius. An applied voltage splits the carbon dioxide into solid carbon and oxygen gas. The carbon deposits on the cathode in the form of carbon nanotubes. The oxygen is released harmlessly back to the atmosphere. The electrolysis cell operates continuously, with carbon nanotubes harvested periodically from the cathode.

The entire system is powered by solar panels. The only inputs are air, sunlight, and makeup sorbent and electrolyte salts which are consumed at very low rates. The outputs are oxygen, pure carbon nanotubes, and the absence of atmospheric carbon dioxide.

4. Module One — Air Contactor

The air contactor is the largest and simplest component of the system. It consists of a housing, a fan, a packing material, and a liquid sorbent circulation system.

Ambient air enters through a coarse filter that removes dust and particulates. A low-speed fan, consuming approximately fifty watts, pushes air through a packed bed of structured packing material. The packing material is corrugated plastic or metal with high surface area, similar to cooling tower fill. The liquid sorbent is sprayed from the top of the packing and flows downward while air flows upward. Carbon dioxide is transferred from the air to the liquid by diffusion and chemical absorption.

The sorbent is a solution of monoethanolamine or similar amine compound. Amines have been used for industrial carbon capture for nearly a century. The chemistry is well understood and non-proprietary. The sorbent concentration is approximately thirty percent by weight in water. The sorbent is recirculated continuously, with a small slipstream sent to the regeneration module.

The size of the air contactor determines the carbon capture rate. A one square meter cross-section contactor with one meter of packing height can capture approximately one kilogram of carbon dioxide per day under average ambient conditions. A ten square meter contactor captures ten kilograms per day. A one hundred square meter contactor, which fits on a typical residential property, captures one hundred kilograms per day, approximately thirty-six metric tons per year.

The air contactor is constructed from common materials. The housing is sheet metal or fiberglass. The packing is polypropylene or stainless steel. The fan is a standard industrial exhaust fan. The pump is a small chemical-resistant centrifugal pump. The total material cost for a one hundred square meter contactor is under five thousand dollars.

5. Module Two — Regeneration and Concentration

The regeneration module releases pure carbon dioxide from the amine sorbent and recovers the amine for reuse.

The carbon dioxide-rich sorbent from the air contactor is pumped into a regeneration column. Low-temperature heat, between one hundred and one hundred twenty degrees Celsius, is applied to the column. This heat breaks the chemical bond between the amine and the carbon dioxide, releasing carbon dioxide gas. The regenerated amine flows back to the air contactor.

The heat source is critical to the efficiency of the system. The CarbonaCore is designed to use waste heat from the electrolysis module as the primary heat source. The molten salt electrolysis cell operates at seven hundred fifty degrees Celsius. Significant waste heat is available at temperatures well above the one hundred twenty degrees needed for amine regeneration. A simple heat exchanger transfers this waste heat to the regeneration module. No additional energy is required.

If the system is operated without the electrolysis module, for example in a configuration that captures and stores compressed carbon dioxide without converting it to nanotubes, a small solar thermal collector can provide the regeneration heat. A few square meters of evacuated tube collectors are sufficient.

The carbon dioxide gas released from the regeneration column is primarily water vapor. The gas passes through a condenser that removes the water. The dry carbon dioxide is then compressed to approximately ten atmospheres, which is the optimal pressure for the electrolysis module. The compressor is powered by the same solar panels that power the rest of the system.

The regeneration module is constructed from standard chemical processing components. The column is stainless steel or fiberglass reinforced plastic. The heat exchanger is copper or stainless steel. The compressor is a standard piston or diaphragm compressor. The total material cost is approximately two thousand dollars.

6. Module Three — Molten Salt Electrolysis

The molten salt electrolysis unit is the heart of the CarbonaCore system. It converts carbon dioxide gas into solid carbon nanotubes and oxygen gas.

The electrolysis cell consists of a high-temperature vessel containing a molten salt electrolyte. The preferred electrolyte is a mixture of lithium carbonate, sodium carbonate, and potassium carbonate, known as ternary carbonate eutectic. This mixture melts at approximately four hundred degrees Celsius. The operating temperature is maintained at seven hundred fifty degrees Celsius to increase reaction kinetics and improve carbon nanotube quality.

Two electrodes are immersed in the molten salt. The cathode is typically nickel or stainless steel. The anode is a dimensionally stable anode coated with iridium or ruthenium oxide to survive the corrosive molten salt environment. Alternative anode materials are under development to eliminate the need for precious metals.

The carbon dioxide gas from the regeneration module is bubbled into the molten salt near the cathode. Carbon dioxide dissolves in the molten salt and forms carbonate ions. When an electric potential of approximately two volts is applied between the electrodes, the carbonate ions are reduced at the cathode, depositing solid carbon, and oxidized at the anode, producing oxygen gas. The overall reaction is carbon dioxide plus electrical energy equals carbon plus oxygen.

The critical innovation is that under the correct conditions of temperature, voltage, current density, and molten salt composition, the carbon deposits not as amorphous soot but as highly ordered carbon nanotubes. The nanotubes grow vertically from the cathode surface, self-aligning into dense forests. The diameter of the nanotubes is controlled by the current density. The length is controlled by the duration of electrolysis.

After a period of several hours to several days, depending on the desired nanotube length, the cathode is removed from the cell. The carbon nanotube forest is harvested by scraping or peeling. The cathode is then returned to the cell for another growth cycle. The process is continuous and produces no waste other than oxygen.

The oxygen gas produced at the anode is vented to the atmosphere. This oxygen is the same oxygen that was originally removed from the atmosphere when the carbon dioxide was captured. Releasing it closes the oxygen cycle and causes no net change. The carbon remains solid and sequestered.

The electrolysis cell requires electrical power at approximately two volts and high amperage. The power consumption is approximately ten kilowatt-hours per kilogram of carbon nanotubes produced. This energy comes from the solar panels. A one hundred square meter air contactor producing one hundred kilograms of carbon dioxide per day requires approximately one kilowatt of solar panel capacity. This is a standard residential solar installation.

The electrolysis cell is constructed from high-temperature alloys. The vessel is stainless steel or Inconel. The heating elements are commercially available cartridge heaters. The power supply is a high-current, low-voltage DC power supply of the type used for electroplating or hydrogen production. The total material cost for a laboratory-scale cell is approximately three thousand dollars. At industrial scale, the cost drops dramatically.

7. Carbon Nanotube Properties and Applications

Carbon nanotubes are cylindrical molecules of pure carbon with diameters measured in nanometers and lengths up to millimeters. The walls are graphene sheets rolled into seamless cylinders. The properties depend on the specific atomic arrangement.

Single-walled carbon nanotubes have a single graphene cylinder. They are flexible, strong, and have tunable electronic properties that can be metallic or semiconducting depending on the rolling angle. Multi-walled carbon nanotubes have multiple concentric cylinders. They are stiffer and more robust but have lower electrical conductivity due to inter-wall interactions.

The CarbonaCore system produces primarily multi-walled carbon nanotubes. These are the most suitable for structural and conductive applications in the P.O.S.T. ecosystem.

The tensile strength of multi-walled carbon nanotubes is approximately fifty gigapascals, compared to half a gigapascal for high-strength steel. The density is approximately one and a half grams per cubic centimeter, compared to eight grams per cubic centimeter for steel. The specific strength, strength divided by density, is approximately sixty times higher for carbon nanotubes than for steel.

The electrical conductivity of carbon nanotubes is up to ten thousand siemens per centimeter, comparable to copper at fifty-nine thousand siemens per centimeter. Unlike copper, carbon nanotubes do not suffer from electromigration at high current densities, making them superior for certain electronic applications.

The thermal conductivity of carbon nanotubes is approximately three thousand watts per meter-kelvin, higher than diamond and more than ten times higher than copper.

These properties make carbon nanotubes valuable for a wide range of applications. Structural composites with carbon nanotubes are lighter and stronger than steel or aluminum. Electrical cables made from carbon nanotubes are lighter than copper and do not suffer from fatigue. Thermal management materials using carbon nanotubes dissipate heat more efficiently than any other material. Batteries and supercapacitors using carbon nanotube electrodes have higher power density and longer cycle life.

The global market price for multi-walled carbon nanotubes ranges from fifty to five hundred dollars per kilogram depending on purity, diameter, and length. The CarbonaCore system produces nanotubes at a material cost of approximately ten dollars per kilogram, primarily the cost of electricity and consumables. The profit margin is substantial even at the lowest market prices.

8. Integration with Existing P.O.S.T. Systems

The CarbonaCore integrates with multiple existing P.O.S.T. systems in a closed loop.

The PyroCore provides waste heat for the amine regeneration module. The PyroCore runs on biomass waste, including the spent substrate from the Food Synthesizer. The waste heat from the PyroCore is normally vented or used for greenhouse heating. The CarbonaCore gives it a higher-value application.

The AquaCore provides ultrapure water for the amine solution and for cooling the electrolysis cell. The AquaCore condenses water from atmospheric humidity, meaning the CarbonaCore system does not require a municipal water connection.

The solar panels that power the CarbonaCore are also the primary power source for the AquaCore and PyroCore. A single solar array can serve all systems simultaneously, with load balancing managed by the Nexus-Core.

The carbon nanotubes produced by the CarbonaCore are used directly in other P.O.S.T. systems. The TerraCore Shelter uses carbon nanotubes as reinforcement for compressed earth blocks, increasing strength and durability. The TerraMobile uses carbon nanotubes as structural material for the chassis and as conductive pathways for the electrical system. The Atomic Forge uses carbon nanotubes as feedstock for the transmutation process, as described in Addendum A, because carbon nanotubes are already pure carbon in a highly ordered crystalline form.

The Nexus-Core monitors and optimizes the CarbonaCore operation. The AI adjusts fan speed, sorbent flow rate, electrolysis temperature and voltage, and nanotube harvest schedule to maximize carbon capture rate and nanotube quality based on current atmospheric conditions and energy availability.

The Judicore verifies the carbon accounting. Each kilogram of carbon nanotubes produced represents approximately three point six seven kilograms of carbon dioxide removed from the atmosphere, because carbon dioxide is twelve parts carbon and thirty-two parts oxygen. The Judicore issues verifiable carbon removal certificates that are cryptographically signed and tamper-proof.

The CarbonaCore is designed to scale from the individual to the planetary level through a fractal architecture.

A single CarbonaCore unit occupies approximately one hundred square meters of land, or less if stacked vertically. It captures approximately thirty-six metric tons of carbon dioxide per year and produces approximately ten metric tons of carbon nanotubes. The capital cost for a single unit, including the air contactor, regeneration module, electrolysis cell, and solar panels, is approximately twenty thousand dollars assuming volume production. The annual revenue from nanotube sales at one hundred dollars per kilogram is one million dollars. The payback period is less than one month.

One million such units, occupying one hundred million square meters, approximately one hundred square kilometers, would capture thirty-six million metric tons of carbon dioxide per year. This is a rounding error relative to the approximately forty billion metric tons of annual global carbon dioxide emissions.

One billion such units, occupying one hundred thousand square kilometers, would capture thirty-six billion metric tons of carbon dioxide per year. This exceeds current global emissions. One hundred thousand square kilometers is approximately the size of Iceland or South Korea. It is less than one tenth of one percent of the Earth's land surface.

The land area required to solve climate change using CarbonaCore units is therefore trivial. The real constraint is manufacturing capacity. A single CarbonaCore unit, combined with an Atomic Forge, can manufacture the components for additional CarbonaCore units. The growth is exponential. Starting from a single seed unit, a fleet of one billion units can be achieved in months rather than decades.

10. Comparison to Existing Carbon Capture Technologies

Climeworks, the leading direct air capture company, uses a solid sorbent that requires heating to one hundred degrees Celsius to release captured carbon dioxide. The energy consumption is approximately two thousand kilowatt-hours per ton of carbon dioxide captured. The captured carbon dioxide is compressed and injected underground. No valuable product is produced. The cost is approximately six hundred to one thousand dollars per ton.

Carbon Engineering uses a liquid sorbent similar to the CarbonaCore but requires heating to nine hundred degrees Celsius for regeneration, which is typically supplied by burning natural gas. The natural gas combustion produces additional carbon dioxide, so the net capture is reduced. The cost is approximately two hundred to six hundred dollars per ton.

The CarbonaCore uses waste heat from electrolysis for regeneration, so the only energy input is solar electricity. The energy consumption is approximately ten kilowatt-hours per kilogram of carbon nanotubes produced, which is equivalent to approximately two thousand seven hundred kilowatt-hours per ton of carbon dioxide captured, similar to Climeworks. However, the revenue from nanotube sales at one hundred thousand dollars per ton far exceeds the energy cost. The net cost is negative. The system makes money while removing carbon.

The only existing technology that converts carbon dioxide directly to carbon nanotubes is being developed by companies like SkyNano and CarbonMeta Technologies. These systems use electrochemical reduction but rely on concentrated carbon dioxide from industrial sources, not direct air capture. They also require expensive catalysts and produce nanotube yields that are not yet commercially viable. The CarbonaCore combines direct air capture with nanotube synthesis in a single integrated system.

11. Known Limitations and Open Problems

The CarbonaCore system has several limitations that must be addressed in ongoing development.

The molten salt electrolyte at seven hundred fifty degrees Celsius is corrosive. The anode requires precious metal coatings to survive extended operation. Iridium and ruthenium are rare and expensive. Research into alternative anode materials, including nickel ferrite and other ceramic oxides, is ongoing. The open-source community can contribute by testing candidate materials and publishing results.

The carbon nanotube growth mechanism in molten salt is not fully understood. The conditions that produce high-quality nanotubes versus amorphous carbon are not completely characterized. Empirical optimization is required. The CarbonaCore design includes sensors for temperature, voltage, current density, and gas composition, allowing the Nexus-AI to perform this optimization automatically across many units.

The amine sorbent degrades over time due to oxidation and thermal decomposition. Typical amine lifetime is one to three years before replacement is required. The degradation products include ammonia and various organic acids. These are minor waste streams that must be managed. Closed-loop recovery of amine degradation products is possible but adds complexity.

The land area requirement for gigaton-scale carbon capture is small but not zero. One hundred thousand square kilometers of land must be allocated to CarbonaCore units. This land could be desert, degraded agricultural land, or rooftop space. No productive land needs to be displaced.

The energy requirement for gigaton-scale carbon capture is substantial. Thirty-six billion metric tons of carbon dioxide captured per year requires approximately one hundred petawatt-hours of electricity per year, which is approximately four times current global electricity generation. However, this energy is solar energy, not fossil energy. The CarbonaCore units include their own solar panels. The land area for the solar panels is already accounted for in the land area for the capture units. No additional grid capacity is required.

The carbon nanotubes produced by the CarbonaCore must be used or stored. The global market for carbon nanotubes is currently tens of thousands of tons per year, not millions or billions of tons. If the CarbonaCore is deployed at climate-relevant scale, the market will be saturated quickly. The excess carbon nanotubes must be stored permanently or used in applications that do not release the carbon back to the atmosphere. Possible applications include construction materials, where carbon nanotubes are embedded in concrete or asphalt and are not released, or deep geological storage, where the nanotubes are buried in a stable formation. The Atomic Forge can also reconfigure carbon nanotubes into graphite blocks for long-term storage.

The economic case for the CarbonaCore is straightforward.

Capital cost per unit: twenty thousand dollars for one hundred square meter unit capturing thirty-six metric tons of carbon dioxide per year and producing ten metric tons of carbon nanotubes.

Annual revenue at one hundred dollars per kilogram nanotube price: one million dollars.

Annual operating cost: negligible. The only consumables are amine sorbent replacement at approximately one hundred dollars per year and electrode replacement at approximately five hundred dollars per year.

Annual net profit: approximately nine hundred ninety-nine thousand four hundred dollars.

Payback period: less than one month.

Return on investment: approximately five thousand percent per year.

Even at a nanotube price of ten dollars per kilogram, far below the current market, the annual revenue is one hundred thousand dollars and the payback period is two to three months.

The carbon removal is free. The profit comes entirely from the value of the nanotubes. If the nanotube market is saturated and prices collapse to the cost of production, approximately ten dollars per kilogram, the system still breaks even while removing carbon. It never requires a subsidy.

This economic analysis assumes volume production of CarbonaCore units drives the capital cost down to twenty thousand dollars. A hand-built prototype will cost more. The first unit may cost one hundred thousand dollars. It will still pay for itself in a few months at current nanotube prices. The profit from the first unit funds the second unit, and so on. Exponential growth is not only physically possible but economically self-sustaining.
